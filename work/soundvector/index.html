The task

A large catalog.
A personal next track.

Turn a song and a listener’s recent choices into a ranked queue.

HNSW retrieves candidates from 899K learned track vectors. Saves and completed previews update decaying taste vectors, while mode-specific weights control the ranking. Recording deduplication and per-artist caps keep the queue from repeating the same release or artist.

The browser shows why each track was selected, connecting the ranking signals to the listening experience.

Why contrastive embeddings?

Songs that share artists, albums and genres land close together, so one nearest-neighbour search covers the whole catalog.

Why a separate ranker?

Mode-specific weights, artist caps and version merging can change the queue without retraining the embedding model.

Why retain sources?

Generated recording notes carry source links when the provider supplies them. Catalog notes remain available when generation is unavailable.

A personal continuation

The same seed song produces different queues for different saved tastes. Saved artists influence the order without replacing the starting song’s context.

Feedback you can undo

Removing a like rebuilds the profile from the remaining likes. Repeated saves do not count the same song twice.

Recording-aware filtering

Dislikes and skips exclude the recording, including provider tracks without a local catalog ID. Live versions and remixes keep their own identities.

Evaluation details and limits

Seeds are 1,000 catalogue songs sampled without replacement with a fixed random seed. Each method returns ten songs: the embedding’s nearest neighbours from the HNSW index, and the ranker in Similar mode.

  • Distinct artists per top 10 rise from 2.77 to 9.0, and songs by the seed’s own artist fall from 7.01 to 1.26.
  • 35.1% of raw-neighbour lists repeat a recording, 0.67 repeats per list on average; none of SoundVector’s do.
  • On the 743 seeds with genre labels, picks sharing the seed’s genre fall from 97.6% to 89.1%. Mean popularity rises from 0.22 to 0.43.
  • The HNSW index returns 99.95% of the exact nearest neighbours (recall@10 over 200 queries).
  • These are queue-composition measures, not a listening study: they show the ranker spreads the queue across artists and releases, not that listeners prefer it.
  • About a quarter of tracks lack a usable artist name, which leaves them outside the artist caps.

The engineering

Everything
around the model.

I built the training pipeline, recommendation engine, API and web client. The engineering connects listener feedback, catalog search and external music metadata.

Diversity as a constraint

Rather than retrain, the ranker merges versions of one recording, caps each artist, rejects candidates above 0.97 cosine similarity to a pick, and widens the candidate pool fourfold until the list fills.

Ranking

Notes with provenance

Generated recording notes retain source links and generation status. Quota errors fall back to catalog notes. A separate rule-based checker flags recognized artist, genre and audio-description mismatches.

Source attribution and fallback handling

Mood queries

Free text such as “sad rainy night” is projected into the embedding space by a TF-IDF and ridge model. An artist name counts only when it matches the whole query, since short names such as Rain once matched inside mood words like “rainy”.

The result

One retrieval engine.
A changing listener.

Personalization updates around a reusable vector index.

Learned representations make the catalog searchable. Decaying taste vectors adapt the query, and a separate ranker applies mode-specific weights, artist caps and recording deduplication. The result is a queue shaped by both the seed track and the listener’s activity.
